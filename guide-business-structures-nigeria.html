Business Name vs. Sole Proprietorship vs. Ltd vs. PLC: Choosing the Right Structure

Before you register anything with the CAC, it helps to actually understand what you're choosing between — because these four terms get thrown around like they're four separate options, when really, they sit in a smaller, more specific relationship than most people realize.

📖 10 min read 🏛 CAMA 2020 🔄 Updated September 2026

First, the one thing most guides get wrong

"Business Name" and "Sole Proprietorship" are not two competing options — a Sole Proprietorship is registered as a Business Name. So is a simple Partnership. "Business Name" is the registration category; Sole Proprietorship and Partnership are the two forms it can take, depending on whether one person or several people own the business.

The real decision most business owners in Nigeria are actually making is between registering a Business Name (as either a sole owner or with partners) or incorporating a company — and if incorporating, whether that company stays private (Ltd) or goes public (PLC).

1. Business Name — Sole Proprietorship

Sole Proprietorship

CAMA 2020, S.814 & Part E

One person trading under a name other than their own full legal name, registered as a Business Name with the Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC). This is the simplest, cheapest, and most common way to start a business in Nigeria.

Merits

  • Fast and inexpensive to register — often 2 to 5 working days
  • Full control — no shareholders or board to answer to
  • Simple tax filing — you pay personal income tax in your state of residence
  • Minimal ongoing compliance or reporting obligations

Demerits

  • Not a separate legal entity — you and the business are legally the same person
  • Unlimited personal liability — your personal assets are exposed if the business owes money
  • Ends when you do — no perpetual succession
  • Harder to raise investment or bring in outside capital
  • Cannot bring in foreign partners or expatriates

2. Business Name — Partnership

Partnership (as a Business Name)

CAMA 2020, S.814 & Part E

Two or more people trading together under one registered name, without forming a formal Limited Partnership (LP) or Limited Liability Partnership (LLP). It works the same way as a sole proprietorship, just with more than one owner sharing the business and its liabilities.

Merits

  • Shared capital, workload, and decision-making from day one
  • Still fast and affordable to register
  • Combined skills and networks of multiple owners

Demerits

  • Still no separate legal identity — same unlimited liability exposure as a sole proprietorship
  • Each partner can be held liable for the actions of the others
  • Disputes between partners have no built-in governance structure to resolve them

3. Private Company Limited by Shares (Ltd)

Ltd

CAMA 2020, Part B, S.18–24

A company is a completely separate legal person from the people who own and run it — it can own property, sign contracts, sue and be sued, all in its own name. Since the CAMA 2020 reforms, even a single person can now incorporate a private company as its sole shareholder and director — something that previously required a minimum of two people.

Merits

  • Limited liability — your personal assets are shielded; risk is capped at what you invested in shares
  • Perpetual succession — the company survives changes in ownership or death of a shareholder
  • Greater credibility with banks, investors, and larger clients
  • Easier to raise funding and bring on investors
  • Can now be formed by just one person (single-member company)

Demerits

  • More expensive and slightly slower to register than a Business Name
  • Minimum issued share capital requirement of ₦100,000
  • Cannot offer shares to the public, and share transfers are restricted
  • Membership capped at 50 shareholders (excluding bona fide employees)
  • More ongoing compliance — annual returns, statutory filings

4. Public Limited Company (PLC)

PLC

CAMA 2020, Part B, S.24–27

A PLC is built for scale — it's the only structure that can invite the general public to buy its shares, which is how companies raise capital on the stock exchange. Its name must end in "PLC" or "Public Limited Company." It carries the most legal obligations of any structure under CAMA.

Merits

  • Can raise capital from the public and list on the stock exchange
  • No cap on the number of shareholders
  • Highest level of credibility and visibility, useful for major national or international deals
  • Shares transfer freely, making ownership more liquid

Demerits

  • Minimum issued share capital of ₦2,000,000 — twenty times the Ltd requirement
  • Must hold a statutory meeting within six months of incorporation
  • Requires a professionally qualified company secretary
  • Must hold an Annual General Meeting (AGM) and meet heavy disclosure/reporting obligations
  • Far more regulatory scrutiny, including from the Securities and Exchange Commission if listed
The honest advice most people need Almost no new business should start as a PLC — it's built for companies that are already large and need public capital. Most founders are really choosing between staying a Business Name a little longer to keep things simple and cheap, or incorporating a Ltd early to protect personal assets and look more credible to banks and bigger clients. If you're handling client money, signing contracts, or taking on any real financial risk, that liability protection usually outweighs the extra cost of incorporating.

Quick decision checklist

  • Working alone, low risk, testing an idea? → Business Name (Sole Proprietorship)
  • Starting with a partner, sharing the workload and risk equally? → Business Name (Partnership)
  • Handling client funds, signing contracts, or want to protect personal assets? → Ltd
  • Planning to raise capital from the public or list on the stock exchange eventually? → PLC (usually after first being a Ltd)
  • Not sure yet? Start as a Business Name — you can always incorporate a Ltd later as you grow.
This guide is for general information only and does not constitute legal advice. Registration requirements, share capital thresholds, and CAC filing fees can change — always confirm current requirements with a qualified Nigerian legal practitioner or directly with the Corporate Affairs Commission before registering.
